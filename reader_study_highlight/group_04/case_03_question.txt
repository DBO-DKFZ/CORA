A <strong>3-year-old boy</strong> is brought to the physician for evaluation of a <strong>generalized, pruritic rash</strong>. The rash <strong>began during infancy</strong> and did not resolve despite initiating treatment with <strong>topical corticosteroids</strong>. <strong>Three months ago</strong>, he was treated for several <strong>asymptomatic soft tissue abscesses</strong> on his <strong>legs</strong>. He has been admitted to the hospital <strong>three times during the past two years for pneumonia</strong>. Physical examination shows a <strong>prominent forehead</strong> and a <strong>wide nasal bridge</strong>. Examination of the skin shows a <strong>diffuse eczematous rash</strong> and <strong>white plaques</strong> on the <strong>face, scalp, and shoulders</strong>. Laboratory studies show a <strong>leukocyte count of 6,000/mm3 with 25% eosinophils</strong> and a <strong>serum IgE concentration of 2,300 IU/mL</strong> (<strong>N = 0–380</strong>). Flow cytometry shows a <strong>deficiency of T helper 17 cells</strong>. The patient’s <strong>increased susceptibility to infection</strong> is most likely due to which of the following?

A. Impaired chemotaxis of neutrophils
B. Impaired DNA repair in lymphocytes
C. Impaired respiratory burst in neutrophils
D. Impaired actin assembly in lymphocytes
E. Impaired Ig class-switching in lymphocytes
F. Impaired interferon-γ secretion by Th1 cells

LLM answer: A. Impaired chemotaxis of neutrophils
