# REVIEW ARTICLE

# Comparison of guidelines and consensus articles on the management of patients with acne with oral isotretinoin

# Abstract

Guidelines and consensus on the management of patients with acne aim to give evidence-based, expert-group recommendations. This review compares current guidelines and consensus articles to provide a compilation of recommendations on the treatment of acne with oral isotretinoin. Ten common, relevant, clinical questions are addressed, based on published recommendations, including the indications of isotretinoin, the proposed daily dose, the cumulative isotretinoin dose and the laboratory monitoring needed. Recommendations on special considerations are also addressed, including the timing of procedures and the question of an association of depression or inflammatory bowel disease with isotretinoin. A major limitation is the use of different classification systems for acne across guidelines. The recommended daily dose ranges from 0.3 to $0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ in the European guidelines to up to $1\mathrm{mg / kg}$ in the US guidelines. A specific duration of treatment of at least 6 months is only recommended in the European guidelines. All guidelines report the need of strict pregnancy prevention measures. The European, French and US guidelines recommend to monitor for symptoms of depression. Important clinical questions that are inconsistently addressed in guidelines include the age indication, the recommendation for a cumulative dose, the timing of procedures, the association of isotretinoin with IBD, the recommendation for preventing acne flares and for appropriate laboratory monitoring. These topics should be clearly included in the recommendations of guidelines as they are often raised in everyday clinical practice.


# Introduction

Oral isotretinoin (13-cis retinoic acid), a natural metabolite of vitamin A, was approved for acne vulgaris in 1982 by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) $^{1}$ and in 1983 by the European Medicines Agency (EMA). $^{2}$ Isotretinoin undergoes rapid intracellular conversion to all-trans retinoic acid and shows strong biological activity and low binding affinity for retinoid receptors. It inhibits sebocytes proliferation, differentiation and lipid synthesis in vivo and in vitro, $^{3,4}$ reduces Propionibacterium acnes, inflammation and hyperkeratinisation in the pilosebaceous unit. $^{5-7}$ The efficacy and safety of oral isotretinoin for acne was reported in the systematic

review by Vallerand et al. $^{8}$ that included 11 randomized controlled trials published up to 2016. There was a clinically significant decrease of acne lesions with isotretinoin and always in a greater extent compared with the control group (placebo, systemic antibiotics or other control arm) in all studies. The frequency of adverse events with isotretinoin was reported to be twice as high compared with the control group. More than half of the adverse events were skin-related and associated with xerosis. Adverse events that led to isotretinoin discontinuation included Stevens-Johnson syndrome, cheilitis, xerosis, acne flare, photophobia, increase in liver enzymes, decrease in appetite, headache and depression. $^{8}$

This review compares current guidelines and consensus articles regarding the recommendations on the treatment of acne

with oral isotretinoin. Guidelines include recommendations aiming to provide optimal care for patients, and they are based on a systematic review of evidence and a critical appraisal of the benefit and risk of available treatment options. $^{9}$ The recommendations on oral isotretinoin for acne include the indications of use, the proposed daily dose, the cumulative isotretinoin dose and the laboratory monitoring during treatment. Also, recommendations on special considerations will be discussed, including whether elective surgical procedures may be performed during treatment and the questions of an association of depression or inflammatory bowel disease with isotretinoin.

# Methods

Published updated international and national guidelines and consensus articles that included recommendations on oral isotretinoin for acne were considered eligible. The EMA recommendations were also included as they provide the proposed framework for licensed drug prescription in Europe. Guidelines published before 2012 or which did not provide any information on their validity date were excluded. Guidelines had to be published in English or French.

The key search words were 'isotretinoin', 'systemic treatment', 'acne', 'guidelines', 'consensus'. The references of included guidelines were also searched for relevant guidelines or consensus. Last search date was 20 May 2019.

# Information of included guidelines and consensus

There were four guidelines: European S3, $^{10}$ French, $^{11,12}$ American Academy of Dermatology (AAD), $^{13}$ and Canadian, $^{14}$ with publication dates ranging from 2015 to 2017. The French guidelines were almost identical to the guidelines by the French Academy of Dermatology, with the difference of not mentioning a cumulative dose. The Canadian guidelines were adapted from the European guidelines (version 2012). $^{15}$

There were 2 international consensus published in 2016 and $2018^{16,17}$ and one US publication with recommendations for paediatric acne in 2013. $^{18}$

Recommendations on issues relevant to clinical practice were compared. In order to provide a more detailed discussion for issues with gaps or differences between the guidelines, a separate section entitled 'Further evidence' is provided at the end of each point, presenting available information from publications with high level of evidence, such as meta-analysis or prospective studies.[19] This statement of facts may allow the critical reader to understand the best way to formulate a treatment approach. Regarding the clinical questions on indications, daily and cumulative dosing, and treatment duration, the comparison of guidelines and consensus is presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The comparison of answers to clinical questions on the timing of procedures, contraception, depression, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and preventing

acne flares is presented in Table 3. The comparison of laboratory monitoring (question 10) is presented in Table 4.

# Classification of acne and acne severity

In most guidelines, recommendations are based on a classification of acne depending on the predominant lesion morphology and the severity. However, the classification used varies among guidelines and consensus and is outlined in Tables 1 and 2.

In the European $^{10}$ and Canadian $^{14}$ guidelines the following classification of acne was used:

- comedonal acne   
- mild to moderate papulopustular acne   
- severe papulopustular acne, moderate nodular acne   
- severe nodular acne, conglobate acne

The French guidelines use the classification of the severity of facial acne according to the Global Evaluation of Acne Scale (GEA) $^{20}$ as:

# Clinical question 1: For which acne forms is systemic isotretinoin indicated?

The indications of isotretinoin for acne and the grades of recommendation given are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

According to the European Directive for prescribing isotretinoin 'oral isotretinoin should only be used in severe acne, nodular and conglomerate acne, that has or is not responding to appropriate antibiotics and topical therapy.'[21] Therefore, oral isotretinoin can only be used as second-line treatment for acne. On the other hand, guidelines and expert consensus articles that are discussed in the following sections mention that there are cases to consider isotretinoin as first-line therapy. However, as it is stressed in the European guidelines, in the event of any medical problems, prescribing clinicians might be deemed liable if they have failed to follow recommended prescribing practice as per the European Directive.[10,15,22]

Current European S3 guidelines (2016) recommend oral isotretinoin with a high strength of recommendation for severe papulopustular/moderate nodular acne and for severe nodular/

conglobate acne. However, it is noted that limitations can apply that may necessitate the use of a treatment with a lower strength of recommendation as a first-line therapy (e.g. financial resources/reimbursement limitations, legal restrictions, availability, drug licensing).<sup>10</sup>

In the Canadian clinical practice guideline (2016), oral isotretinoin is strongly recommended as first-line therapy for severe acne, with a high-strength recommendation. The strength of these recommendations was adapted from the European S3 guidelines (2012).<sup>15</sup> Efficacy, safety, level of evidence and patient preference were considered in determining the strength of recommendations. High-strength recommendation was defined as action strongly recommended; definitely use. It is noted that in view of the potential for adverse events and teratogenicity, prescribing of oral isotretinoin should be limited to physicians who are trained and experienced in its use, monitoring and appropriate pregnancy-prevention measures.<sup>14</sup>

In the good practice guidelines for the treatment of acne of the French Society of Dermatology (2015) $^{11}$ and the French guidelines (2017), $^{12}$ oral isotretinoin is recommended as first line treatment for very severe acne (GEA 5), corresponding to inflammatory acne that covers the entire face with nodules. For moderate acne (GEA 3) and severe acne with rare nodules (GEA 4), oral isotretinoin is recommended as second-line treatment after failure of a 3-month therapy with oral antibiotics

For severe acne, it is mentioned (with no grade of recommendation given) that oral isotretinoin might be started in case of failure of other first-line therapy before the completion of 3 months, in case of significant risk of scarring or in case of rapid relapse (Table 1).

The guidelines of the American Academy of Dermatology recommend isotretinoin for the treatment of severe nodular acne and suggest that it is appropriate for the treatment of moderate acne that is treatment-resistant or for the management of acne that is producing physical scarring or psychosocial distress (consensus).<sup>13</sup>

It is not possible to draw a clear conclusion for the indication of isotretinoin for acne because there are inconsistent recommendations across guidelines and consensus. A major limitation is the discrepant use of classification systems for acne across guidelines (Table 1).

# Clinical question 2: At what ages is treatment indicated?

According to EMA, isotretinoin is not indicated for the treatment of prepubertal acne and is not recommended in patients $< 12$ years of age. The European guidelines are conformed with these recommendations; however, experts agreed that an age under 12 years does not exclude obligatorily isotretinoin administration.[15]

Evidence-based recommendations for paediatric acne were published in 2013. The consensus article recommended isotretinoin for severe, scarring and/or refractory acne in adolescents (strength of recommendation A: based on consistent and good-quality patient-oriented evidence) and stated that it may be used in younger patients (strength of recommendation C: based on consensus, usual practice, opinion, disease-oriented evidence or case series for studies of diagnosis, treatment, prevention or screening).<sup>18</sup> It was also recommended to provide extensive counselling on isotretinoin teratogenicity and pregnancy avoidance and to monitor carefully for possible adverse events.<sup>18</sup> It was noted that the majority of clinical trials for acne medications are conducted in patients 12 years of age or older, and there is not sufficient evidence for acne treatments in paediatric patients<sup>18</sup> (Table 2).

# Further evidence

Similarly, a recent systematic review mentioned that all published randomized controlled trials for isotretinoin in acne included patients older than 12 years of age.[8]

Acne may present at an earlier age than 12 years, including neonates, infants, mid-childhood or prepubertal children. However, acne in these younger age groups is not usually severe to require oral isotretinoin. Neonatal acne is a self-limiting condition, infantile acne usually responds to topical anti-acne agents or systemic antibiotics for more severe types, acne in mid-childhood (between 1 and 7 years of age) is very rare and hyperandrogenism should always be ruled out.[23] Rare patients with recalcitrant infantile acne associated with scarring that needed isotretinoin have been reported. It is outside the scope of this review to discuss in detail these cases that have been reviewed elsewhere.[23]

A limitation in the prescribing indication of isotretinoin by EMA is the definition of 'puberty' at age 12. Acne in prepubertal age may be the first physical sign of pubertal maturation, and thus, the presence of severe acne should raise a suspicion of possible endocrine disorders such as premature adrenarche, congenital adrenal hyperplasia or true precocious puberty.[24-26] A retrospective US study in the National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey database over the period from 1979 to 2007 reported a significant decrease in the mean age of children seeking treatment for acne. They reported that these results support the increasingly earlier onset of puberty in children.[27]

# Clinical question 3: Which is the recommended daily dosing?

The EMA recommends to start isotretinoin at a dose of $0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ while the dose may range from 0.5 to $1.0~\mathrm{mg / kg}$ .

The European guidelines mention that best dosage evidence is rare and partly conflicting, whereas recommendations were based more on expert opinion. For severe papulopustular acne/moderate nodular acne, systemic isotretinoin $0.3 - 0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ can be recommended. For conglobate acne, a dosage of $\geq 0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ can be recommended. The duration of treatment should be at least 6 months, and in case of insufficient response, it can be prolonged.[10,15]

Similar recommendations are given in other published guidelines and consensus articles that are summarized in Tables 1 and 2.

Recommendation that isotretinoin must be taken with food to be efficiently absorbed is given in the Canadian guidelines Appendix $4^{14}$ and the AAD guidelines. $^{13}$

The French $^{12}$ and the AAD guidelines $^{13}$ do not recommend intermittent or low-dose isotretinoin.

Regarding the need of once or twice daily dosing, there are no recommendations given in any of the guidelines $^{10,12-14}$ or consensus articles. $^{16-18}$

# Further evidence

Thirty minutes after oral intake, oral isotretinoin is detectable in the blood and maximum concentrations are reached $2 - 4\mathrm{h}$ later.[28] Oral isotretinoin is highly lipophilic, and its bioavailability can be increased 1.5-2 times by intaking with fat components (milk, butter, cheese, oil) during or after a meal.[29,30]

# Clinical question 4: Is a cumulative dose necessary?

The concept of cumulative dose (mg/kg bodyweight), introduced by Harms in 1989, is the total amount of oral isotretinoin taken by the patient over the entire duration, divided by the bodyweight.[31,32] According to the EMA, a treatment course of 16-24 weeks is normally sufficient to achieve remission and it is mentioned that no substantial additional benefit is to be expected beyond a cumulative treatment dose of $120 - 150\mathrm{mg}/$ kg.[2]

The European guidelines state that the evidence on cumulative dose is rare and partly conflicting. A cumulative dose of isotretinoin is not recommended; instead, the duration of therapy with isotretinoin is recommended to be at least 6 months, and in case of insufficient response, the treatment period can be prolonged (expert opinion). [10,15] (Table 1).

In the international consensus by Thiboutot et al.,[17] it is recommended to proceed with treatment until full clearance of acne plus one additional month and that additional studies are needed to define a total cumulative dose (if any) that maintains remission.

On the other hand, the AAD guidelines support a goal cumulative dose between 120 and $150\mathrm{mg / kg}$ [13]

# Further evidence

Relapses after oral isotretinoin treatment may occur in 39-45.1% of patients and 16-29% of patients may need 2 or 3 courses (retries) of isotretinoin.[33-38] Also, relapse rates depend on the duration of follow-up and they are higher during the first 2 years after isotretinoin therapy.[33,35] Factors reported in various study that may influence the risk of relapse include younger age of acne onset,[36,38,39] male sex,[38] truncal acne,[3] family history of acne[39] and prepubertal acne[39] and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)[40,41] A cumulative dose of $< 120\mathrm{mg / kg}$ has been associated with acne relapse[33] or acne retrial[34] while this was not confirmed in another retrospective study.[36] A systematic review (2015) reported that evidence underpinning a cumulative dosing of $120 - 150\mathrm{mg / kg}$ was equivocal and based on two low-grade studies. Furthermore, it was mentioned that the definitions for acne relapse and treatment endpoints varied considerably across studies.[42] Relapse has been defined as recurrence of acne sufficient to merit systemic therapy[33] or acne treated by prescription anti-acne treatment,[38,43] or recurrence of acne of a grade equal to or greater than 2.[36]

A nested case-control study reported that a cumulative dose $>2450\mathrm{mg}$ was less likely to be associated with acne relapse.38 Another prospective single-center study investigated the rate of relapse with cumulative dose $>220~\mathrm{mg / kg}$ . Patients with severe nodular, refractory acne were treated with isotretinoin until there were no new acne lesions for 1 month. At 12-month post-treatment follow-up, patients were questioned on possible acne

relapse. Patients were classified into two groups, those that reached a cumulative dose $< 220 \mathrm{mg/kg}$ and those that reached a cumulative dose $\geq 220 \mathrm{mg/kg}$ . After adjustment for confounding factors (age, sex, duration of treatment), there was no statistically significant difference in the rate of relapse between the two groups (43.8% vs. 26.6%, $P = 0.22$ ); the authors commented that this may have been due to small sample size and that the difference in relapse rates was clinically significant.[43] However, if the study was underpowered due to small sample size, the results are not robust.

# Clinical question 5: What are the recommendations for the timing of procedures with isotretinoin treatment?

The association between isotretinoin and abnormal wound healing remains controversial, which leads to the question of postponing elective procedures to a time point after discontinuation of isotretinoin treatment. A comparison of recommendations in guidelines and consensus articles is presented in Table 3.

According to the EMA recommendations, aggressive chemical dermabrasion and cutaneous laser treatment should be avoided for at least 6 months after the end of the treatment because of the risk of hypertrophic scarring in certain body areas and more rarely postinflammatory hyper- or hypopigmentation. Also, wax depilation should be avoided for at least a period of 6 months after treatment because of the injuring result of epidermal stripping risk. The European guideline group supported these recommendations; however, they stated that this area may be open for future review. They did not identify any evidence to support the avoidance of laser therapy, wax epilation and peeling for at least 6 months after isotretinoin treatment; however, they acknowledged that isotretinoin could interfere with wound healing.

The AAD guidelines 2016 state that, while elective procedures should be delayed for 6-12 months when possible, careful consideration may be given on a case by case basis.[13] The American Society of Dermatologic Surgery (ASDS) Guidelines Task Force in 2017 concluded that there is insufficient evidence to justify delaying treatment with superficial chemical peels and non-ablative lasers, including hair removal lasers and lights, vascular lasers and non-ablative fractional devices for patients currently or recently exposed to isotretinoin (GRADE of recommendation B: moderate). They reported that focal or superficial manual dermabrasion may also be safe when performed by a well-trained clinician. However, full-face dermabrasion and mechanical dermabrasion with rotary devices were not recommended within 6 months of isotretinoin use.[44]

The French and the Canadian guidelines do not provide any recommendation on this topic. $^{12,14}$

# Further evidence

According to a systematic review published in 2017, 'the inconsistencies in the reported data and the methodological flaws in the literature preclude any firm conclusion that can resolve the

controversy. $^{45}$ A critical appraisal of the literature (2014) on whether isotretinoin should be stopped prior to surgery (including dermabrasion, laser, wisdom tooth extraction) reported the limited amount of evidence in the literature and also the lack of controls does not allow to reach any clear recommendations. They concluded that 'there is some doubt that isotretinoin could interfere with wound healing, but overall the risk described in the larger and better-reported studies was relatively small or absent.' $^{46}$ A study reported that among 25 patients treated with isotretinoin that had a wisdom tooth extraction, three patients $(11.4\%)$ reported a dry socket postoperatively. $^{47}$

In a current review, Mysore et al.48 analysed the studies published on isotretinoin and wound healing and concluded that the recommendation made earlier about the need to avoid dermatosurgical procedures in patients on isotretinoin is based on inadequate and insufficient evidence and hence needs revision. The review also suggested that recent studies on the subject establish that performing such procedures is safe.

A systematic review and consensus recommendation by Spring et al. reported that there was insufficient evidence to support delaying manual dermabrasion, superficial chemical peels, cutaneous surgery, laser hair removal and fractional ablative and non-ablative laser procedures for patients currently receiving or having recently completed isotretinoin therapy. It was mentioned that additional high-quality prospective studies are recommended. Based on the available literature, mechanical dermabrasion and fully ablative laser were not recommended in the setting of systemic isotretinoin treatment. It was proposed to have an evidence-based discussion with patients informing about the known risk of cutaneous surgical procedures in the setting of systemic isotretin.49

# Clinical question 6: Which is the essential duration of contraception after discontinuation of isotretinoin treatment?

Oral isotretinoin is teratogenic, and it should only be prescribed to women of childbearing potential under strict pregnancy prevention measures.[2,12] For male patients, there is no evidence to suggest that the fertility or offspring of male patients will be affected by them taking isotretinoin.[2]

The good practice recommendations of the French Society of Dermatology (2015) $^{11}$ include recommendations for prevention of pregnancy measures, such as:

Before treatment onset, women of childbearing potential should:

- Be informed and understand the teratogenicity of isotretinoin and the absolute need to avoid any pregnancy,   
- Read carefully and sign an informed consent form for treatment and contraception and keep one copy,   
- Use effective contraception for at least 4 weeks before treatment onset,

- Have a negative serum pregnancy test result within 3 days before first prescription,

During treatment and for at least 1 month after the end of treatment with oral isotretinoin, women of childbearing potential should:

- Take effective contraception measures during treatment and for at least one month after the end of treatment,   
- Have a serum pregnancy test every month, within 3 days before each monthly prescription of isotretinoin,   
- Have a serum pregnancy test at 5 weeks after the end of treatment.

Similar strict prevention of pregnancy recommendations are given by the EMA and the European and AAD guidelines. In addition, in USA the strict pregnancy prevention program iPLEDGE is mandatory.[13]

# Clinical question 7: Is isotretinoin associated with depression?

The EMA mentions that psychiatric disorders have been reported in patients treated with isotretinoin, including depression (rare, $\geq 1/10000$ and $< 1/1000$ ), and suicide/suicide attempt/abnormal behaviour/psychotic disorder (very rare, $\leq 1/10000$ ). It is mentioned that particular care needs to be taken in patients with a history of depression and all patients should be monitored for signs of depression. Discontinuation of isotretinoin may be insufficient to alleviate symptoms and further psychiatric or psychological evaluation may be necessary.[2]

The European Guidelines state that there is continued uncertainty regarding isotretinoin, depression and suicidal behaviour and that caution and patient information appears reasonable. It is mentioned that the current literature comes to different conclusions and that there were important limitations in studies. They recommend to assess prior symptoms of depression in the patient's medical history before isotretinoin onset and during treatment and to inform patients about a possible risk of depression and suicidal behaviour.[10]

The French guidelines mention that no available population-level data support that isotretinoin increases the risk of depression in, or suicide attempts by, patients suffering from acne. However, it is stated that a rare individual risk could not be excluded and that before initiation of isotretinoin treatment, the patient and his/her family must be informed of the potential risk of psychiatric disorders and the treating physician must be notified of any mood or behaviour change.[12]

The AAD guidelines (2016) mention that, although mood changes, including depression, and suicidal behaviour have been reported sporadically, there are no studies to suggest an evidence-based link between isotretinoin and depression, anxiety, mood changes, or suicidal ideation/suicide. However, it is proposed that prescribing physician should continue to monitor for these symptoms and make therapeutic decisions within the context of each individual patient.[13]

The consensus article of Eichenfield et al.,[18] conclude that ongoing vigilance and surveillance of patients for mood changes while on isotretinoin therapy seem reasonable but the data appear reassuring. The topic of depression was not addressed in the consensus articles of Gollnick et al.[16] and Thiboutot et al.[17]

# Further evidence

Published meta-analyses report some conflicting results. The meta-analysis of Li et al., in 2019 reviewed the literature up to December 2017 and reported 17 prospective studies with results on depression symptoms scores before and after treatment with isotretinoin for acne. A meta-analysis of these studies reported significant improvement in the grading of depression symptoms with isotretinoin compared with the status before therapy. Four studies reported results on the association of isotretinoin with the development of depression. The meta-analysis of two retrospective studies reported statistically significant increased risk of depression with isotretinoin, while on the contrary the meta-analysis of two prospective studies did not find a statistically significant risk of depression.[50]

The systematic review of Vallerand et al. on the adverse events of oral isotretinoin for acne included 11 studies and mentioned that psychiatric or psychosomatic adverse events were approximately $50\%$ more frequent with isotretinoin compared with controls (32 vs. 19 cases, respectively), although no diagnosis was confirmed by a psychiatrist. The meta-analysis of Huang et al. included 31 studies published up to September 2016 and reported that isotretinoin did not appear to be associated with increased risk of depression. On the other hand, they reported improvement of depression symptoms after treatment for acne (isotretinoin or other treatment).

These conflicting results may be attributed at least in part, to the different risk of psychological sequelae of acne in adolescents or to the psychological effects of acne (especially severe acne) per se and the lack of a randomized controlled study investigating these associations with isotretinoin.[50,51]

# Clinical question 8: Is there an association of oral isotretinoin with IBD?

According to the summary of product characteristics of isotretinoin, 'isotretinoin has been associated with inflammatory bowel disease (including regional ileitis) in patients without a prior history of intestinal disorders.'[52]

According to the French guidelines, a high level of evidence and concordant data support that isotretinoin does not increase the risk of IBD.[12]

The US work group guidelines (2016) state that the current evidence is insufficient to prove either an association or causal relationship between isotretinoin use and IBD.[13]

The European S3 Guidelines do not mention any recommendation on the association of oral isotretinoin with IBD. $^{10}$ The Canadian guidelines provide some information from publications investigating the relationship between the isotretinoin and development of IBD in the Appendix 4 (full guideline). It is mentioned that an inquiry about IBD symptoms and relevant family history, along with discussion of the controversy, would be prudent prior to initiating therapy with isotretinoin. However, they do not provide any recommendation on this topic. $^{14}$

There are neither recommendations provided in the consensus articles of Gollnick et al., Thiboutot et al. or Eichenfield et al. $^{16-18}$ (Table 3).

# Further evidence

The risk of IBD with isotretinoin was investigated in a meta-analysis that included studies published up to July 2015 and that compared the development of inflammatory bowel disease in adult patients exposed to isotretinoin with that in controls.[53] The meta-analysis included two retrospective cohort studies and four case-control studies, with a total of 9724 069 participants. After pooling data and meta-analysis for patients that were exposed to isotretinoin compared with controls, there was neither statistically significant risk of IBD (OR 1.08, $95\%$ CI 0.82-1.42, $P = 0.59$ ) nor individually for Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis.[53]

# Clinical question 9: What are the recommendations for preventing acne flares during isotretinoin treatment?

A flare (exacerbation) of acne may occur in up to $15\%$ of acne patients during the initial period of isotretinoin intake.[17,54] The topic of acne flares and how to prevent them is inconsistently addressed across guidelines and consensus articles (Table 3).

According to the French guidelines, comedone extraction may be proposed before isotretinoin onset in order to decrease the risk of severe inflammatory flares at treatment initiation (expert consensus). For numerous and very severe comedones, isotretinoin should also be initiated at a lower dose of $0.2-0.3\mathrm{mg/kg/day}$ in order to decrease the risk of acute and severe exacerbation (acne fulminans) (expert consensus) (Table 1).

In the consensus articles of Thiboutot et al. and Eichenfield et al., it is recommended to start with a low dose $(0.5\mathrm{mg / kg})$ for the first 4 weeks to avoid initial flares and then increasing to the full dosage of $1\mathrm{mg / kg}$ per day.[17]

# Clinical question 10: What are the recommendations for laboratory monitoring during treatment with isotretinoin?

European Medicines Agency recommends laboratory testing, mainly of lipids and liver enzymes, before treatment initiation

and at month 1 and 3 thereafter. It is mentioned that monitoring may be more frequent in selected patients with co-morbidities.[2]

The European guidelines recommend to monitor laboratory parameters, primarily liver enzymes and lipids, before treatment, 1 month after starting treatment, and every 3 months thereafter, in line with the EMA recommendation.

Published guidelines do not give detailed recommendations on laboratory monitoring during isotretinoin treatment.[12,14,15] The recommendations for laboratory monitoring (apart from pregnancy testing) during isotretinoin treatment are summarized in Table 4. The decision for laboratory monitoring is based on the judgement of the treating physician in relation with the risk factors, the medical history and the symptoms of each patient.

# Further evidence

A systematic review and meta-analysis (2016) investigated published studies reporting on laboratory monitoring for acne for lipids, hepatic panel and complete blood counts during isotretinoin treatment. Isotretinoin treatment was reported to be associated with statistically significant changes in the mean value of laboratory tests (white blood cells, hepatic and lipid panel); however, these mean changes were not considered as high-risk and there was a small proportion of patients with abnormal laboratory tests.[55] The authors concluded that monthly laboratory testing is not supported for all acne patients who receive standard doses of isotretinoin.[55]

A retrospective study in 515 patients that received 574 isotretinoin cycles, reported leucopenia in $2.4\%$ of treatment cycles, without needing to discontinue treatment. There was clinically non-significant thrombocytopenia in $1.6\%$ of treatment cycles and increase of alanine aminotransferase in $3.3\%$ of treatment cycles without the need to discontinue isotretinoin. Lipid abnormalities were more frequent. The majority of laboratory abnormalities $(67.7\%)$ was detected within the first 60 days of treatment.[56] The authors suggested to check the family and personal medical history for lipid, liver or hematologic disorders. For healthy patients that are treated with isotretinoin for acne, it was proposed to have laboratory testing for lipids and liver function at baseline and repeat it at month 2, after maximum daily dosing of isotretinoin is reached. If tests are normal at month 2, the authors proposed that no further testing may be required. If there are abnormalities or if there is a known lipid abnormality, then further testing will be needed. For complete blood cell counts, the authors proposed that routine testing is not recommended, unless there is a known disorder or risk factor (Table 4).[56]

This was considered a practice-changing study, as it suggests that there is excessive laboratory monitoring in patients with acne treated with isotretinoin (overmonitoring). As a result, unnecessary blood draws and health costs may be avoided.[56,57]

# Comments and conclusions

Guideline recommendations aim to improve patient care based on the existing scientific evidence of best available quality at the time of their writing, regarding effectiveness and safety of treatments. However, there are limitations in the existing evidence and guidelines. As mentioned in the disclaimer of the US work group guidelines, 'adherence to these guidelines will not ensure successful treatment in every situation. Furthermore, these guidelines should not be interpreted as setting a standard of care, or be deemed inclusive of all proper methods of care, nor exclusive of other methods of care reasonably directed to obtaining the same results. The ultimate judgement regarding the propriety of any specific therapy or technique must be made by the physician and the patient in light of all the circumstances presented by the individual patient, and the known variability and biologic behaviour of the disease.'[13] Lastly, the European guidelines mention that in the event of any medical problems, clinicians could be deemed liable if they have failed to follow recommended prescribing practice.[15]

Another publication evaluated acne guidelines published from 2013 to December 2016 regarding their quality using the AGREE II checklist and their trustworthiness as defined by the Institute of Medicine criteria. Acne treatment guidelines were deficient in several key areas, and it was proposed to improve them in line with the increasingly stringent requirements for guideline development.[9]

Keeping that in mind, it is noteworthy that there are considerable differences across current guidelines. An important unifying starting point would be to use a common classification system for acne. It is not possible to draw a clear conclusion for the indication of isotretinoin for acne, because there are inconsistent recommendations across guidelines and consensus. A major limitation is the discrepant use of classification systems for acne across guidelines. The recommended daily dose ranges from 0.3 to $0.5\mathrm{mg / kg}$ in the European guidelines, to up to $1\mathrm{mg / kg}$ in the US guidelines. A specific duration of treatment of at least 6 months is recommended only in the European guidelines and of 16-24 weeks in the consensus of Gollnick et al. All guidelines report the need of strict pregnancy prevention measures. The European, French and AAD guidelines recommend to monitor for symptoms of depression. Important clinical questions that are inconsistently addressed in guidelines include the age indication, the recommendation for a cumulative dose, the timing of procedures, the association of isotretinoin with IBD, the recommendation for preventing acne flares and for appropriate laboratory monitoring. These topics should be included in the recommendations of guidelines as they are often raised in everyday clinical practice.

The differences in recommendations for acne management as well as the limitations in the methodological quality of included

studies underline that unanswered clinical questions still exist and that there is room for standardization and further improvement of guidelines.[58]
