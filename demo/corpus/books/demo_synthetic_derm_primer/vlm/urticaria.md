## Clinical features

Urticaria presents with transient, intensely pruritic weals that individually resolve
within 24 hours and leave no residual pigmentation. Angioedema, a deeper swelling of
the dermis and subcutis, accompanies weals in roughly 40% of cases in this demo
chapter. Chronic spontaneous urticaria is defined by weals, angioedema, or both
recurring for more than six weeks without an identifiable external trigger.

A lesion that persists at the same site beyond 24 hours, burns rather than itches, or
resolves with bruising or pigmentation argues against urticaria and raises the
possibility of urticarial vasculitis, in which case a lesional biopsy is indicated.

## Pharmacotherapy

The recommended first-line pharmacotherapy for chronic spontaneous urticaria in this
demo chapter is a **second-generation H1 antihistamine at the standard licensed
dose**. First-generation sedating antihistamines are not recommended as first-line
therapy because of sedation and anticholinergic effects.

Stepwise escalation:

1. Second-generation H1 antihistamine at standard dose for two to four weeks.
2. If symptoms persist, increase the same antihistamine up to fourfold the standard
   dose.
3. If symptoms still persist, add **omalizumab**, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody.
4. Ciclosporin is considered for omalizumab-refractory disease.

Systemic glucocorticoids are limited to short courses of no more than ten days for
acute exacerbations and have no role in long-term control.
